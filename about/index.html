The definition

The risk metric answers one question: relative to its own history, is this asset expensive or cheap? It is risk-v1, and it is three steps.

One — separation from trend. Take the logarithm of the daily close and subtract the logarithm of its own 365-day average. This is a measure of how far price has run from its own recent trend, in a space where a doubling and a halving are the same size.

Two — the diminishing correction. Multiply that separation by the day index raised to a fixed power. Log deviations from trend shrink cycle over cycle as an asset matures, so without this the 2013 extremes swamp everything after them and the metric reads “nothing has been risky since 2013” forever. The exponent is a constant of the definition, which is exactly why the definition is versioned.

Three — expanding-window normalisation. Scale the result into 0 to 1 against the running minimum and maximum of the days before it. Not the whole history. This is the step almost every published version of this chart gets wrong, and it is the reason this site exists.

Why the third step is the whole product

Scale a metric between its all-time minimum and maximum and every point in 2015 is drawn using the range of 2021 — a number nobody could have computed at the time, on a chart whose entire purpose is telling you what to do today. It renders perfectly. Every historical value on it is a claim about the past made with information from the future.

Ours expands: each day is scaled only against the days before it, so every value plotted for a past date could have been computed on that date. The chart is uglier, the backtests are worse, and the early years have to be dropped entirely rather than shown against a range of one. It is the only version that means anything.

Where it misleads

Four of these, stated here rather than in a help article, because a metric whose failure modes are undocumented is a metric that will be reported as a bug by the people it misleads.

It does not top when price tops

The metric peaks when separation from the 365-day mean peaks, and that is weeks or months away from the price high. February 2021 reads 0.917; the April 2021 price high reads 0.809. Both are correct and the gap is the definition working as specified — a sharp run away from a slow-moving average is a more extended market than a higher price the average has since caught up with. It is not a bug and it will not be fixed.

The first years are missing on purpose

An expanding window has almost no window at the start. The first thousand days are dropped from every display of the metric rather than shown, because a value normalised against forty prior observations is arithmetic, not information.

It is relative, and only relative

0.9 means extended against this asset's own history. It is not a probability, not a price target and not a signal. An asset can sit near 1 for months, and has. Anything that reads as a forecast on this site is a market-implied probability — a price other people are paying, labelled as such — never the risk metric.

The drawn line is smoothed; the model is not

Charts plot a 14-day mean of the daily value, because the raw series is noisy enough to be unreadable. That smoothing is a display constant shared by every surface — chart, table, band histogram — so the site cannot show two different numbers for today. The definition itself returns the raw daily series and always will.

The study that cost us a tier

A 2026 paper reports that repricing in prediction-market macro contracts forecasts crypto volatility. It was going to be the flagship: a $2,000–10,000 a month data tier, and the headline feature of the plan below it.

We replicated it on our own data first. It did not hold — t = −0.16 against the paper's 3.63 over 313 overlapping days, and out of sample the signal made the forecast worse (MSFE ratio 1.039). Sixty-six regressions were run and all sixty-six are reported, including the eight that survived multiple-testing correction and then vanished the moment a time trend was added.

So the node is not built, the tier is deleted, and the plan below it is priced lower than it was going to be. A price list is the first place a failed replication should show up. The alternative — charging the original price for the same page with the best thing removed — was available, easy, and would never have been discovered from outside.

Four things this will never do

  • Never custody funds. There is nothing here to deposit into and there never will be.
  • Never match users with a venue. No brokerage links, no referral codes, no order flow.
  • Never take paid placement. The settlement-trust scores are the most monetisable thing on the site and the most corruptible; a venue cannot buy its way up them, and the day one could, the whole measurement is worthless.
  • Never give investment advice. Nothing here is a recommendation. It is research, and the difference is not a formality.

Who is behind the numbers

Deliberately, this section is short and it is last. A name at the top of a risk model is a weaker guarantee than a published definition, a version history you can read, and a study we printed against our own interest — and the first two of those are checkable by a stranger in about five minutes.

What is offered instead of a credential: every metric definition is on this site with its constants, every change to one is dated in the transparency notice, every chart carries the version that produced it, and the replication study that killed a revenue tier is published in full rather than summarised. If any of that turns out to be wrong, it is wrong in public and in writing.

The work is independent, funded by the plans on the pricing page and by nothing else — no outside capital, no venue relationships, no affiliate revenue. Correspondence reaches a person rather than a queue: the contact page.

What is actually here today

83 distinct charts across 14 assets (439 generated asset views), all readable without an account — Bitcoin from 2010, the S&P 500 from 1927, gold from 1968. Prediction-market probabilities from public endpoints. Settlement-trust scores across 132,154 markets on both major venues, which is the one measurement here that nobody else publishes at any price.

Everything is daily close. Free data lags by hours, and no part of this site implies otherwise.
